Arium Tournament Redesign

Designing a tournament experience players could understand as it changed.

I worked on Arium’s desktop and mobile tournament experience in a three-person design team. A tournament could change while a player was using it, so joining, checking results, and reading the rankings could not rely on one static screen. We designed the experience around those changing moments and documented the behaviour needed to build it.

Role

UX/UI Designer

Duration

3 months

Team

Three designers

Platforms

Desktop and mobile

Scope

Tournament experience

The problem

A tournament could change before a player finished reading it

A review of the experience and directional prototype testing pointed to four moments that needed work: taking action, finding the terms of the tournament, following a match, and making sense of the standings.

Actions that matched the state

Show what a player could do alongside the tournament condition that made that action relevant.

Details at the decision point

Bring rules, rewards, and schedules into the moments when a player was deciding whether to join or follow.

Useful match rows

Give schedules and results enough context to help players follow a match or open an available stream.

Rankings that could flex

Make room for different tournament structures while showing more about the players in the table.

My role

My contribution ran from early structure to handoff

I proposed a way to select and estimate the design work, helped refine the tournament flow, and contributed to the journey map and final UI. I also created early feature structures and interactive prototypes, then proposed and produced component-state documentation for the developer handoff.

Direct contribution

Planning structure, flow refinement, early feature organisation, interactive prototypes, UI contribution, and component-state documentation for handoff.

Collaboration

Three-person design team, with input from product, leadership, technical stakeholders, and developers.

Key design decisions

Four decisions made the changing parts easier to follow

Rather than treating the tournament as a single page, we focused on what a player needed at four points in the experience: action, context, match progress, and standings.

01

Make the next action depend on the tournament state

We treated the CTA area as a component with states, so the available action appeared with the condition that explained it.

Tournament action states

Each variant pairs the action with the tournament condition a player needs to understand before acting.

The CTA changes as the tournament does.

The component shows how the available action changes with tournament status and participation.

02

Put the terms of the tournament where players make decisions

We brought rules, rewards, schedules, and other key details into the tournament flow instead of leaving them apart from it.

Tournament details in context

Details are available without sending the player away from the tournament.

The grouping keeps the information needed to understand the tournament alongside the experience itself.

Rules, rewards, and schedules sit within the tournament experience.

03

Let schedules and results do more than list a time or score

We used match rows to carry a summary and a direct route to an available stream.

Match context and stream access

A match row carries both progress and a route to watch.

Players can use the same row to understand what happened and, when available, open the match content.

Match rows combine tournament progress, a short summary, and stream access.

04

Keep rankings recognisable while allowing for different formats

We reorganised the ranking area so it could support different tournament types and show more about the players involved.

Adaptable ranking display

The ranking pattern differentiates tournament types while giving players more context for interpreting a position.

The structure develops from an early sketch into a flexible ranking pattern.

The ranking design adapts to different tournament formats without becoming a different experience each time.

Final direction

One flow, from finding a tournament to reading the standings

The desktop and mobile designs connect discovery, tournament details, participation, match progress, and rankings. The handoff also makes the conditional behaviour behind those screens explicit.

Design output and learning

What the work delivered—and what it did not measure

Because tournament-specific post-launch metrics were not isolated, broader Arium growth figures are not attributed to this redesign.

Design output

The completed direction connected tournament actions, details, match context, and rankings. The component documentation captured the intended behaviour for implementation.

Evidence limit

The project record does not isolate a tournament-specific post-launch metric, so broader Arium growth figures are excluded from this case study.

Learning

I learned that complex features need more than polished screens: they need explicit behaviour, information at the right moment, and a shared model that design and engineering can work from.

Next validation step

Validate with external players and tournament-specific measures

With more time, I would measure registration completion, repeat participation, schedule usage, and ranking comprehension.
