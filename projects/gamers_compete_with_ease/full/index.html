Arium Tournament Redesign

Designing a tournament experience players could understand as it changed.

Tournaments introduced a changing experience into Arium. A player might be deciding whether to join, checking a match, or reading the standings while tournament status and their own participation changed around them. I worked with a three-person design team to make those moments easier to follow—from early structure and prototypes to UI refinement and handoff documentation.

Product context

Competitive play added a changing layer to Arium

Cloud access reduced the hardware barrier to play. The tournament experience added another challenge: its interface had to respond to both the tournament’s status and a player’s participation without making the competition hard to follow.

The work was not a UI refresh

A tournament is not one page to browse. A player needs to find a competition, understand its terms, act at the appropriate time, follow progress, and make sense of the rankings. Those needs change with the tournament and with the player’s place in it.

The design challenge

The problem was knowing what mattered at each moment

The tournament did not lack information. What was missing was a reliable way to show the right action, terms, match context, and ranking information at the time a player needed it.

01 · Friction

Actions became difficult to interpret as tournament status or player participation changed.

What that meant for the design — the available action needed to appear with the context that explained it.

02 · Friction

Rules, rewards, schedules, and other key details were difficult to access.

What that meant for the design — players needed the important terms before deciding to join or follow.

03 · Friction

Schedules and results lacked direct access to match summaries and streams.

What that meant for the design — a match row needed to support both checking progress and opening available content.

04 · Friction

Ranking views did not clearly support different tournament types.

What that meant for the design — the ranking pattern needed to adapt without becoming unfamiliar each time.

05 · Friction

Ranking tables provided limited information about other players.

What that meant for the design — the standings needed more context about the players behind the positions.

My role and contribution

My contribution ran from early structure to developer handoff

I worked as a UX/UI Designer alongside a design lead and another designer. The final direction was collaborative. My contribution was to help structure the work, explore and test weak points, refine the UI, and document how the feature should behave beyond a static screen.

My direct contribution

I proposed the UX checklist, helped the Product Manager refine the flow, created early feature structures and interactive prototypes, contributed to UI improvements, and proposed and created the component-state handoff documentation.

Design team collaboration

The three-person design team collaborated on competitor review, workshops, prioritization, journey mapping, iteration, final UI, and design reviews.

Cross-functional collaboration

The Product Manager, Product Owner, CEO, technical team, developers, and stakeholders contributed requirements, constraints, feedback, and implementation review.

Design strategy

The strategy was to explain the system, not remove its depth

The team used five working principles to decide where the experience needed more explanation for players and more explicit behaviour for developers.

Show the action with its context

Let the action and its supporting information respond to tournament status and participation together.

Keep the terms close to the choice

Place rules, rewards, schedules, and tournament information where a player is deciding to join or follow.

Make a match row useful

Connect schedules and results with a summary and available stream access.

Adapt without starting over

Use ranking patterns that can work across games and tournament formats without becoming unfamiliar.

Specify behaviour as well as appearance

Document component states, interactions, variations, and ranking behaviour as carefully as the final screens.

Final solution

Four decisions shaped the player’s path through a tournament

The final direction focused on four connected decisions in the tournament experience. The value described below is what the design was intended to support, not a measured behavioural outcome.

1. State-aware tournament actions

Evidence

Action buttons and supporting information became difficult to interpret when tournament conditions changed.

What we changed

We designed the CTA area so its action and supporting information respond to tournament condition and participation.

Why it mattered

The relevant action and its context appear together, reducing how much a player has to infer.

The component variants show how the action and explanation change with a player’s status in the tournament.

2. Tournament details inside the core experience

Evidence

Rules, rewards, schedules, and other key details were difficult to access.

What we changed

We brought these details into the tournament feature and organised them with a clearer information hierarchy.

Why it mattered

Players can find the information needed to understand the competition where they are deciding to join or follow.

Rules, rewards, and schedules are part of the tournament experience rather than a separate destination.

3. Match context inside schedules and results

Evidence

Players needed easier access to match summaries and available streams from schedules and results.

What we changed

We expanded match rows to include a game summary and a direct link to watch the match.

Why it mattered

The table can support both checking tournament progress and moving into available match content.

Match info & stream links

Added match info & stream links

4. More adaptable and informative rankings

Evidence

Ranking views did not clearly support different tournament structures and provided limited player information.

What we changed

We reorganised the ranking area to differentiate tournament structures and include more player detail.

Why it mattered

Players have more context for interpreting the competition, while the pattern can work across games and formats.

The final desktop and mobile designs connect tournament action, key details, match progress, and rankings in one player journey.

Design output, limits, and reflection

The work delivered a direction; its product impact was not isolated

The available sources support a qualitative design output, not a quantified product outcome. I have not attributed broader Arium growth figures to this redesign.

Design output

The final direction connected actions to status and participation, brought rules, rewards, and schedules into the flow, added match context, created adaptable rankings, and documented behaviour for implementation.

Validation limit

Directional testing with gamer colleagues identified immediate confusion. The record does not confirm an external sample, task-success data, or a tournament-specific post-launch measurement.

What I would measure next

Registration completion, abandonment by state, repeat participation, schedule and stream usage, and ranking comprehension with external players.

Reflection

A polished screen is not enough for a changing feature

This project reinforced a lesson I carry into complex product work: the UI needs explicit state logic, information at the right moment, and documentation that gives design and engineering the same model of how the experience should behave.
