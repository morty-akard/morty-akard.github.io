Making market research easier to create

47% less time to set up market research in a usability evaluation

As Product Designer, I redesigned BetaTrends’ creation flow to bring editing, preview and pricing together in one workspace, then refined it through two rounds of usability testing.

Project
BetaTrends New Interaction
Role
Product Designer
Period
2023–2024
Collaborator
Pieter, founder and product owner

I was responsible for the creation-flow redesign, usability testing and design handoff.

I worked with Pieter, BetaTrends’ founder and product owner, and the design and development team. Pieter and I selected the structural direction together.

BetaTrends helped creators collect audience feedback on their products. To launch a research activity, they had to work through a long form and move between editing, preview and pricing.

Context: An early-stage market-research platform in Australia. The team needed a practical creation experience for a soft launch while the product and its shared design system were developing.

Turning audience feedback into product decisions

BetaTrends let creators ask an audience questions and receive reports of their responses. Each research activity was called an Interaction.

My work focused on setting up an Interaction: preparing content and questions, defining the audience, reviewing cost and checking the preview.

A long form with disconnected preview and pricing

Creators entered their research details in one long form, then opened separate windows to check the cost and preview. The flow gave them little guidance about where they were or what remained.

Choosing what to deliver first

Illustrative dialogue

Visible steps did not solve the loss of context

Before the soft launch, I recommended reviewing product usage through Hotjar. After the limited launch, I reviewed recordings and analysed user and customer feedback with the team.

The findings pointed to three needs: seeing the audience-facing preview while creating, understanding how choices affected cost, and distinguishing research questions from eligibility questions.

I grouped the findings around effort, clarity and continuity, then discussed them with Pieter and the team and facilitated workshops to explore another structure.

The next design needed to keep steps, editing, preview and estimated cost together.

Looking beyond visible steps

Illustrative dialogue

Bringing editing, preview and pricing into one workspace

I explored comparable products and developed three layouts that combined editing, preview and pricing. Pieter and I chose the layout shown below, considering familiar navigation, clear progress, working space and feedback from our workshops. We accepted a denser layout to keep progress, editing, preview and pricing visible together.

Selected direction

What changed and why

  • Preview and pricing during editing. Creators could check the audience-facing result and estimated cost without leaving their work.
  • Space for editing and preview. I moved the creation steps to the left, adjusted spacing and made the main sidebar expand on hover.
  • Distinct question sections. I separated research questions from eligibility screening so creators could distinguish what they wanted to learn from who could participate.

Comparing the directions

Illustrative dialogue

Testing revealed three usability problems

I chose a Figma prototype to avoid adding load to the live product, then conducted a usability test through Maze before development. I wanted to understand whether creators could follow the flow, manage questions and complete the setup.

We recruited through BetaTrends’ existing user network. Some participants completed the test independently; I supported and observed others remotely, combining those observations with Maze’s recorded results.

What the first test revealed

  • Difficulty navigating between questions, editing them and adding another.
  • Similar buttons made the next action unclear.
  • Missing required information could be discovered only at Finalise.
Problems found in the first test

Illustrative summary

Making questions easier to manage

I changed the question forms to an accordion so creators could open a question for editing and collapse it when reviewing the set. I also designed drag-to-reorder. Pieter initially deferred reordering; it was eventually released.

I adapted the accordion within our shared MUI-based design system and documented the component changes for developers.

Making the next action clearer

I made Continue the clear next action by regrouping controls in the Preview area and reducing Back’s emphasis. I removed the separate Finalise button; creators could still reach Finalise from the last step or the phase icon above.

Showing unfinished work earlier

I added distinct icons, labels and colours for incomplete steps. I specified that a step should show as complete only when required information was provided, and that Finalise should remain unavailable while required information was missing.

I updated the prototype and conducted a second Maze round to evaluate the refinements.

Retesting showed fewer misclicks

After refining question management, actions and completion feedback, I tested the updated prototype again. The second Maze report recorded fewer misclicks and shorter average task times across the same seven task areas.

Task area Measure First round Second round
Questions and Eligibility Questions Misclick rate 77% 21.1%
Preview and Finalise Misclick rate 56.8% 2.3%
Preview and Finalise Average task time 121.7 sec 15.7 sec

The first Maze round had 10 participants and the second had 22. Samples and test conditions differed, so these comparisons do not isolate the effect of the design changes.

From retesting to release

After the second Maze round, I handed the final Figma screens and end-to-end prototype to developers, including question editing, incomplete-step states and the rules for entering Finalise. The redesigns were released.

After release, we continued reviewing Hotjar recordings and in-person feedback.

What I learned

Simplifying a complex workflow does not always mean removing steps.

Visible progress, timely feedback and an in-context preview mattered alongside the sequence. I learned the value of testing early and revisiting a direction when evidence challenged it.
